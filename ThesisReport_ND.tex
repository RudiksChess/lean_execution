\begin{abstract}
This chapter presents a complete, machine-checked proof of the completeness theorem for
classical propositional natural deduction, formalized in Lean~4 and verified to depend on
\emph{no} unproved assumptions beyond the standard classical foundations of the Lean/Mathlib
ecosystem. We fix a surface language with atoms, negation, and implication; equip it with the
standard classical (truth-table) semantics; and define a genuine natural-deduction calculus
$\Gamma \Provnd \varphi$ with contexts, hypothesis discharge, and a classical reductio rule.
We then prove \emph{soundness} (every derivable formula is a tautology) and \emph{completeness}
(every tautology is derivable from the empty context). Completeness is established
\emph{internally}, by a constructive realization of \emph{Kalm\'ar's lemma}: for each fixed
valuation we build a derivation from the literal context determined by that valuation, and we
then discharge the finitely many atoms one at a time by a classical case split. No external
``oracle'' and no library completeness theorem is used in the proof; the role of the
\emph{Foundation} library is relegated to an independent cross-validation appendix. The
exposition is organized so that every step appears first as ordinary mathematics with a full
proof, and then as its line-by-line Lean realization. We close with an axiom audit
(\texttt{\#print axioms}) confirming that the completeness theorem reduces exactly to
\texttt{propext}, \texttt{Classical.choice}, and \texttt{Quot.sound}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation and relation to Stansifer (2001)}
\label{sec:stansifer}

The guiding inspiration for this chapter is Ryan Stansifer's paper
\emph{Completeness of Propositional Logic as a Program}~\cite{Stansifer2001}.
Stansifer emphasizes a viewpoint that has become central in modern mechanized reasoning:
a constructive completeness proof can be executed as a program that takes a tautology $\varphi$
and returns a derivation of $\varphi$ in a fixed proof system.

In Stansifer's development, propositional formulas use only $\Not$ and $\Imp$, and proofs are
explicit proof trees in a Hilbert system (specifically \L{}ukasiewicz axioms plus modus ponens).
The program produces proof objects and is correct by construction. The same conceptual
foundation appears throughout proof theory and type theory: \emph{proof objects are computational
artifacts} and can be manipulated systematically.

Lean provides a setting where this slogan is made precise: proofs are typed terms, and the
verification of correctness is delegated to a small trusted kernel. This chapter adopts
Stansifer's conceptual perspective but in the setting of \emph{natural deduction} rather than a
Hilbert calculus, and it carries out the completeness argument \emph{internally}: the derivation
of each tautology is built from the genuine introduction/elimination rules, with no appeal to an
external decision procedure or an imported completeness theorem. The proof strategy is the
classical \emph{Kalm\'ar argument}~\cite{Mendelson2015,Kalmar1935}, which is naturally
``executable'' in Stansifer's sense: given the truth table of $\varphi$, it produces a concrete
derivation.

\section{Why Lean is a good fit (a short primer)}
\label{sec:lean-primer}

This section explains what Lean is checking, why the approach is trustworthy, and how to read the
code as mathematics. The reader already comfortable with proof assistants may skim to
Section~\ref{sec:syntax}.

\subsection{Proofs as typed terms (Curry--Howard)}
Lean is based on dependent type theory. The key operational principle is the Curry--Howard
correspondence:
\[
\text{Propositions} \ \leftrightarrow\  \text{Types},
\qquad
\text{Proofs} \ \leftrightarrow\  \text{Terms (programs)}.
\]
Under Curry--Howard, an implication $P\Imp Q$ at the \emph{meta-level} corresponds to a function
type $P \to Q$: a proof of $P\Imp Q$ is a function transforming proofs of $P$ into proofs of $Q$.
This is the conceptual bridge between natural deduction and typed functional programming. For a
systematic treatment, see S{\o}rensen--Urzyczyn~\cite{SorensenUrzyczyn2006} and
Pierce~\cite{PierceTAPL2002}.

\begin{remark}[Two levels of logic]
\label{rem:two-levels}
It is essential to keep two layers separate throughout this chapter.
\begin{itemize}
\item The \emph{meta-level} (or ambient) logic is Lean's own type theory. When we \emph{prove a
theorem about} our calculus---for instance, ``soundness holds''---we reason in this ambient logic,
and Lean's built-in connectives ($\land,\lor,\to,\neg,\forall$) and tactics live here.
\item The \emph{object-level} logic is the propositional calculus we are studying. Its formulas
are data (an inductive type \texttt{Formula}), and its provability relation
$\Gamma \Provnd \varphi$ is an inductively defined predicate. An object-level ``proof'' is a piece
of structured data, not a Lean function.
\end{itemize}
The completeness theorem is a meta-level statement \emph{about} the object level: ``for every
object-level formula that is semantically valid, there exists an object-level derivation.''
\end{remark}

\subsection{Inductive definitions as inference systems}
Lean's \texttt{inductive} definitions encode the \emph{least} set closed under the specified
constructors. This is exactly how a finite list of inference rules generates a provability
relation. An inductive predicate
\[
\mathrm{ND} : \mathsf{Set\ Formula} \to \mathsf{Formula} \to \mathsf{Prop}
\]
whose constructors are the inference rules gives a fully formal notion of derivability: any Lean
term of type \texttt{ND $\Gamma$ $\varphi$} \emph{is}, by construction, a derivation of $\varphi$
from $\Gamma$. Because the type is the \emph{least} such closed family, we automatically obtain an
induction principle---``rule induction''---which is the formal counterpart of induction on the
height of a derivation. Every proof by induction on a derivation in this chapter is, in Lean,
exactly this generated recursor.

\subsection{Computation inside proofs}
Lean definitions are computational: they can be unfolded and reduced. The \texttt{simp} tactic
performs definitional unfolding and rewriting with registered lemmas. In this chapter \texttt{simp}
is used mainly to expand the recursive definition of \texttt{eval} and to discharge elementary
membership facts about finite sets; the logically substantive steps are always written out
explicitly with named rules.

\subsection{What ``verified'' means here, precisely}
A Lean development is trustworthy to the extent that (i) the kernel accepts every term, and
(ii) the statement one reads is the statement one means. Point (i) is enforced automatically.
Point (ii) is a human responsibility, which is why this chapter pairs every Lean declaration with
its mathematical statement. Finally, Lean tracks which logical \emph{axioms} a theorem ultimately
rests on; the command \texttt{\#print axioms} reports them. Section~\ref{sec:axioms} uses this to
certify that our completeness theorem uses no \texttt{sorry} (no admitted gaps) and no bespoke
``oracle'' rule---only Lean's three standard classical axioms.

\section{The internal logic: the Calculus of (Inductive) Constructions}
\label{sec:cic}

This section makes precise what the Lean kernel manipulates when it accepts our proofs. The point
is to remove any mystery from later phrases such as ``by induction on the derivation'' or ``the
completeness term'': each is a concrete operation in a well-understood type theory. A reader who
internalizes the dictionary in \S\ref{sec:cic-dict} can read every subsequent Lean block as the
literal construction of the mathematical object under discussion.

\subsection{From the Calculus of Constructions to Lean's kernel}
Lean's kernel implements a dependent type theory in the lineage of the \emph{Calculus of
Constructions} (CoC) of Coquand and Huet~\cite{CoquandHuet1988}, extended to the \emph{Calculus of
Inductive Constructions} (CIC) by Coquand and Paulin-Mohring~\cite{CoquandPaulin1990}; this is the
same family of systems underlying Coq, and Lean~4's precise variant is documented by
Carneiro~\cite{Carneiro2019}.

The essentials we rely on are these.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Dependent function types.} The fundamental type former is the dependent product
$(x : A) \to B\,x$ (written $\forall x : A,\ B\,x$ when $B\,x$ is a proposition). It subsumes both
ordinary functions $A\to B$ (when $B$ does not depend on $x$) and universal quantification.
\item \textbf{Propositions as types.} There is a distinguished universe $\mathsf{Prop}$ (also
$\mathsf{Sort}\,0$). A proposition is a type living in $\mathsf{Prop}$, and a proof of it is a term
of that type. $\mathsf{Prop}$ is \emph{impredicative}: $\forall (P:\mathsf{Prop}),\,\dots$ is again
a proposition. Data, by contrast, lives in the predicative hierarchy $\mathsf{Type}\,u$.
\item \textbf{Inductive definitions (the ``I'' in CIC).} One may declare new inductive types. Each
declaration extends the theory with a type former, its \emph{constructors}, and a generated
\emph{eliminator} (recursor) embodying structural induction/recursion. CoC alone can only
\emph{encode} inductive types weakly; CIC makes them primitive, which is what lets us define both
the syntax \texttt{Formula} and the provability relation \texttt{ND} directly.
\item \textbf{Lean-specific refinements.} $\mathsf{Prop}$ is \emph{definitionally proof-irrelevant}
(any two proofs of one proposition are interchangeable), there is definitional $\eta$, and the
kernel supports quotient types. The classical principles we use---excluded middle in case splits,
and the choice implicit in defining \texttt{lit} by a proposition-valued test---are justified by
three axioms (\texttt{propext}, \texttt{Classical.choice}, \texttt{Quot.sound}); these, and only
these, are reported by the audit in \S\ref{sec:axioms}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Inductive types: constructors and the eliminator}
\label{sec:cic-elim}
When we write (Section~\ref{sec:nd})
\[
\texttt{inductive ND : Set Formula} \to \texttt{Formula} \to \mathsf{Prop}
\]
with the seven constructors \texttt{hyp},\,\dots,\,\texttt{classical}, the kernel adds to the
environment: the family \texttt{ND} itself; the seven constructors (these \emph{are} the
introduction rules, each a $\Pi$-type returning \texttt{ND \dots}); and an eliminator \texttt{ND.rec}.
The eliminator's type \emph{is} the induction principle for derivations. Schematically (suppressing
the implicit contexts):
\begin{lstlisting}
ND.rec :
  (C : (Γ : Set Formula) → (φ : Formula) → ND Γ φ → Prop) →   -- the motive
  (case_hyp : …) → (case_impI : …) → … → (case_classical : …) → -- one per rule
  ∀ {Γ φ} (d : ND Γ φ), C Γ φ d
\end{lstlisting}
Two properties make this exactly ``induction on the height of a derivation.''
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Least fixed point.} Because the type is \emph{generated} by its constructors, the
only closed inhabitants of \texttt{ND $\Gamma$ $\varphi$} are finite trees of constructor
applications. Hence establishing the motive $C$ for every constructor establishes it for
\emph{all} derivations: there are no others. This is the formal content of rule induction.
\item \textbf{Elimination into $\mathsf{Prop}$.} All our motives are themselves $\mathsf{Prop}$-valued
(soundness's $\forall v,\ (\dots)\to\sem{\varphi}_v$; Kalm\'ar's pair of implications), and
eliminating a $\mathsf{Prop}$-inductive into $\mathsf{Prop}$ is always permitted. Whenever the text
says ``by induction on the derivation,'' Lean applies \texttt{ND.rec} with the stated motive; the
surface syntax \texttt{induction d with | hyp \dots | impI \dots} is sugar for precisely this.
\end{itemize}
The same story holds for syntax: \texttt{Formula} is an inductive type with recursor
\texttt{Formula.rec}; ``induction on $\varphi$'' in Kalm\'ar's lemma is \texttt{Formula.rec}, and
the recursive definitions \texttt{eval}, \texttt{atoms}, \texttt{tr} are compiled to it.

\subsection{The dictionary: object logic versus ambient type theory}
\label{sec:cic-dict}
Remark~\ref{rem:two-levels} distinguished the object level from the meta level; in CIC terms the
correspondence is exact.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Mathematical / logical notion} & \textbf{CIC counterpart in Lean} \\
\midrule
Object formula $\varphi$ & term of the inductive type \texttt{Formula} (in $\mathsf{Type}$) \\
Context $\Gamma$ & term of \texttt{Set Formula} $=$ \texttt{Formula $\to$ Prop} \\
Derivability judgment $\Gamma\Provnd\varphi$ & the proposition \texttt{ND $\Gamma$ $\varphi$} (in $\mathsf{Prop}$) \\
A particular derivation & a \emph{term} \texttt{d : ND $\Gamma$ $\varphi$} (a constructor tree) \\
An inference rule & a constructor ($\Pi$-type returning \texttt{ND \dots}) \\
A meta-theorem (lemma, soundness, completeness) & a $\Pi$-type, e.g.\ $\forall\varphi,\ \texttt{IsTautology }\varphi \to \texttt{ND }\emptyset\,\varphi$ \\
A proof of a meta-theorem & a term inhabiting that $\Pi$-type \\
``Induction on a derivation'' & application of the eliminator \texttt{ND.rec} \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The decisive line is the third-from-top: \emph{a derivation is a term}. An object-level proof is
not described, asserted, or imported---it is built, as a finite data structure, and the kernel
checks it has the claimed type. A meta-theorem such as completeness is likewise a term, of the
displayed $\Pi$-type; constructing and type-checking that term \emph{is} proving the theorem.

\subsection{How to read the rest of this chapter}
Each subsequent section carries a short \emph{Internal view (CIC)} note naming the mechanism the
Lean code uses---constructor application, \texttt{ND.rec}, \texttt{Formula.rec}, or the inhabiting
$\Pi$-term. These notes let the reader verify, at every step, that the code constructs exactly the
object the mathematics describes, with no hidden gap and no appeal to an external authority.

\section{Preparatory background: natural-deduction rules and their Lean realizations}
\label{sec:prep-doc}

This section prepares the reader to interpret Lean proofs as natural-deduction derivations. The
emphasis is on the \emph{structural correspondence} between the familiar proof trees of symbolic
logic and the Lean expressions that inhabit the corresponding types. The connectives discussed
here ($\land,\lor$) are Lean's \emph{meta-level} connectives (Remark~\ref{rem:two-levels}); they
are the tools we use to reason \emph{about} the object calculus, whose own primitive connectives
are only $\neg$ and $\to$.

\subsection{From informal reasoning to formal rules (why proof trees)}
Informal mathematical reasoning is organized into reusable patterns: ``if $\dots$ then $\dots$,''
``either $\dots$ or $\dots$,'' ``assume $\dots$ and derive a contradiction.'' Symbolic logic
isolates these patterns by replacing content-rich statements with propositional variables and
English connectives by formal connectives. Inference then becomes the study of a small number of
\emph{rules}, drawn as proof trees: if $C$ is derivable from $A$ and $B$, we write
\[
\frac{A \qquad B}{C}.
\]
In natural deduction, trees may contain \emph{subproofs} under temporarily assumed hypotheses that
are later \emph{discharged} (canceled). This is the formal counterpart of hypothetical reasoning:
``Assume $A$; derive $B$; therefore $A\to B$, with $A$ discharged.''

\subsection{The central idea: propositions as types, proofs as terms}
At the meta-level, Lean makes the proof-tree discipline executable:
\[
\text{a proposition }P \text{ is a type}, \qquad \text{a proof of }P \text{ is a term of type }P.
\]
Proving $P$ means constructing a term of type $P$; the kernel checks well-typedness, and that
check \emph{is} the correctness criterion. Implication is realized as a function type, so
modus ponens is function application and implication introduction is lambda abstraction.

\subsection{Rules of inference as proof trees (meta-level illustrations)}
We illustrate the correspondence using Lean's built-in connectives. (In our object calculus only
$\neg$ and $\to$ are primitive; the rules below show how Lean reasons.)

\subsubsection{Implication elimination (modus ponens)}
\begin{prooftree}
\AXM{$A \to B$}
\AXM{$A$}
\RLM{$\to\mathrm{E}$}
\BIM{$B$}
\end{prooftree}
If \texttt{h1 : A $\to$ B} and \texttt{h2 : A}, then \texttt{h1 h2 : B}: modus ponens is
application.

\subsubsection{Implication introduction (hypothetical reasoning)}
\begin{center}
\begin{prooftree}
\AXM{}
\RLM{1}
\UIM{$A$}
\noLine
\UIM{$\vdots$}
\noLine
\UIM{$B$}
\RLM{$1\;\;\to\mathrm{I}$}
\UIM{$A \to B$}
\end{prooftree}
\end{center}
The label ``1'' marks the hypothesis $A$ discharged at the end. In Lean this is a lambda:
if assuming \texttt{h : A} we can build \texttt{B}, then \texttt{fun h : A => \dots} has type
\texttt{A $\to$ B}.

\subsubsection{Negation, falsity, and \emph{ex falso}}
At the meta-level, negation is implication into \texttt{False}: $\neg A \equiv (A \to \bot)$. Thus
negation elimination
\begin{center}
\begin{prooftree}
\AXM{$\neg A$}
\AXM{$A$}
\RLM{$\neg\mathrm{E}$}
\BIM{$\bot$}
\end{prooftree}
\end{center}
is again application (\texttt{hnot hA : False}), and \emph{ex falso}
\begin{center}
\begin{prooftree}
\AXM{$\bot$}
\RLM{$\bot\mathrm{E}$}
\UIM{$A$}
\end{prooftree}
\end{center}
is \texttt{False.elim}. The same three shapes---application, abstraction, elimination of
falsity---reappear as constructors of our object-level calculus in Section~\ref{sec:nd}.

\subsection{Where proof trees appear in our development}
Proof trees appear in two tightly related ways.
\paragraph{(1) As inductive objects.} The constructors of \texttt{ND} \emph{are} the object-level
inference rules; a term \texttt{d : ND $\Gamma$ $\varphi$} is an object-level derivation tree.
\paragraph{(2) As terms built by tactics.} When we prove a \emph{meta-theorem} (soundness,
completeness, a structural lemma) with \texttt{by \dots}, Lean's tactic mode constructs a
meta-level proof term. The tactic script is a readable recipe for that term.

\section{Surface syntax and semantics (\texttt{Syntax.lean})}
\label{sec:syntax}

\subsection{Mathematics}
Fix a countable set $\mathsf{Var}$ of propositional variables (in the implementation, strings).
\begin{definition}[Formulas]
The set of formulas is generated by the grammar
\[
\varphi ::= p \;\mid\; \Not\varphi \;\mid\; (\varphi\Imp\psi),\qquad p \in \mathsf{Var}.
\]
\end{definition}
We use only $\Not$ and $\Imp$ as primitive; this is the same minimal fragment as
Stansifer~\cite{Stansifer2001}, and it suffices for classical propositional logic since the other
connectives are definable.

\begin{definition}[Valuation and evaluation]
A \emph{valuation} is a function $v : \mathsf{Var} \to \{\mathsf{T},\mathsf{F}\}$ (in Lean, a
function into \texttt{Prop}). Evaluation $\sem{\cdot}_v$ is defined by recursion on syntax:
\[
\sem{p}_v = v(p),\qquad
\sem{\Not\varphi}_v = \Not\,\sem{\varphi}_v,\qquad
\sem{\varphi\Imp\psi}_v = \big(\sem{\varphi}_v \Imp \sem{\psi}_v\big).
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Tautology]
$\varphi$ is a \emph{tautology}, written $\mathrm{Taut}(\varphi)$, iff $\sem{\varphi}_v$ holds for
every valuation $v$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[On encoding truth values as \texttt{Prop}]
We take \texttt{Valuation := String $\to$ Prop}, so an atom's value is a Lean proposition rather
than a Boolean. The two presentations agree under classical logic, which we enable locally with
\texttt{Classical.propDecidable}; the recursive clauses for $\Not$ and $\Imp$ then use Lean's own
$\neg$ and $\to$ at the meta-level. This keeps \texttt{eval} definitionally transparent to
\texttt{simp}.
\end{remark}

\section{The natural-deduction calculus (\texttt{NaturalDeduction.lean})}
\label{sec:nd}

\subsection{Encoding falsity inside the \texorpdfstring{$\{\Not,\Imp\}$}{neg,imp} fragment}
Because the object language has no primitive $\bot$, we represent falsity by a canonical formula
that is false under every valuation.

\begin{definition}[Object falsity]
Fix a distinguished atom $p_0$ (in the code, the string \texttt{"$\bot$"}). Define
\[
\bot \;:=\; \Not(p_0 \Imp p_0).
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[$\bot$ is semantically false]
\label{lem:evalBot}
For every valuation $v$, $\;\sem{\bot}_v \leftrightarrow \mathrm{False}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$\sem{p_0\Imp p_0}_v = (v(p_0)\Imp v(p_0))$ holds (the identity implication is true under any
truth value). Hence $\sem{\Not(p_0\Imp p_0)}_v = \Not\,\mathrm{True} = \mathrm{False}$. Conversely
$\mathrm{False}$ implies anything.
\end{proof}

\lstinputlisting[linerange=bot-bot]{Thesis/Prop/NaturalDeduction.lean}

\subsection{Mathematics: the rules}
The judgment is $\Gamma \Provnd \varphi$, where $\Gamma$ is a \emph{set} of formulas. We write
$\Gamma, \varphi$ for $\Gamma \cup \{\varphi\}$ (in Lean, \texttt{insert $\varphi$ $\Gamma$}). The
rules are the standard natural-deduction rules for the $\{\Not,\Imp\}$ fragment, together with a
classical reductio rule.

\begin{center}
\begin{prooftree}
\AXM{$\varphi \in \Gamma$}
\RLM{hyp}
\UIM{$\Gamma \Provnd \varphi$}
\end{prooftree}
\qquad
\begin{prooftree}
\AXM{$\Gamma,\varphi \Provnd \psi$}
\RLM{$\Imp\mathrm{I}$}
\UIM{$\Gamma \Provnd \varphi\Imp\psi$}
\end{prooftree}
\qquad
\begin{prooftree}
\AXM{$\Gamma \Provnd \varphi\Imp\psi$}
\AXM{$\Gamma \Provnd \varphi$}
\RLM{$\Imp\mathrm{E}$}
\BIM{$\Gamma \Provnd \psi$}
\end{prooftree}
\end{center}

\begin{center}
\begin{prooftree}
\AXM{$\Gamma,\varphi \Provnd \bot$}
\RLM{$\Not\mathrm{I}$}
\UIM{$\Gamma \Provnd \Not\varphi$}
\end{prooftree}
\qquad
\begin{prooftree}
\AXM{$\Gamma \Provnd \Not\varphi$}
\AXM{$\Gamma \Provnd \varphi$}
\RLM{$\Not\mathrm{E}$}
\BIM{$\Gamma \Provnd \bot$}
\end{prooftree}
\qquad
\begin{prooftree}
\AXM{$\Gamma \Provnd \bot$}
\RLM{$\bot\mathrm{E}$}
\UIM{$\Gamma \Provnd \varphi$}
\end{prooftree}
\qquad

\AXM{$\Gamma,\Not\varphi \Provnd \bot$}
\RLM{RAA}
\UIM{$\Gamma \Provnd \varphi$}
\end{prooftree}

absurdum}, called \texttt{classical} in the code) is what makes the calculus classical and is
exactly what is needed for completeness with respect to the classical truth-table semantics.

\section{Structural and derived rules}
\label{sec:derived}

Before soundness and completeness we record three lemmas used repeatedly. Each is a meta-theorem
proved by rule induction or by exhibiting a derivation.

\subsection{Weakening (monotonicity in the context)}
\begin{lemma}[Weakening]
\label{lem:weak}
If $\Gamma \Provnd \varphi$ and $\Gamma \subseteq \Delta$, then $\Delta \Provnd \varphi$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By induction on the derivation of $\Gamma \Provnd \varphi$, generalizing over $\Delta$. We treat
each rule.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{hyp:} from $\varphi\in\Gamma$ and $\Gamma\subseteq\Delta$ we get $\varphi\in\Delta$,
so \texttt{hyp} gives $\Delta\Provnd\varphi$.
\item \textbf{$\Imp\mathrm{E}$, $\Not\mathrm{E}$, $\bot\mathrm{E}$:} these rules keep the context
unchanged in their premises. Apply the induction hypotheses with the same inclusion
$\Gamma\subseteq\Delta$, then reapply the rule.
\item \textbf{$\Imp\mathrm{I}$, $\Not\mathrm{I}$, RAA:} these have a premise over an extended
context, e.g.\ $\Gamma,\varphi$. From $\Gamma\subseteq\Delta$ we obtain
$\Gamma,\varphi\subseteq\Delta,\varphi$ (monotonicity of \texttt{insert}). Apply the induction
hypothesis to the extended inclusion, then reapply the rule.
\end{itemize}
\end{proof}

\lstinputlisting[linerange=weakening-weakening]{Thesis/Prop/NaturalDeduction.lean}

\noindent The seven \texttt{|}-branches mirror the seven cases of the proof exactly;
\texttt{insert\_subset\_insert} is the Mathlib lemma $\Gamma\subseteq\Delta \Rightarrow
(\Gamma,\chi)\subseteq(\Delta,\chi)$ used in the three discharge cases.

\paragraph{Internal view (CIC).} \texttt{induction d} elaborates to \texttt{ND.rec} applied with
the motive
\[
C\,\Gamma\,\varphi\,(\_ : \texttt{ND }\Gamma\,\varphi)\ :=\ \forall\,\{\Delta\},\ \Gamma\subseteq\Delta \to \texttt{ND }\Delta\,\varphi .
\]
Note that the motive is itself a $\Pi$-type (it quantifies over $\Delta$); this is why the proof
``generalizes over $\Delta$'' before inducting. Each branch supplies the corresponding minor
premise of \texttt{ND.rec}, and---being able to use the induction hypotheses \texttt{ih}---rebuilds
the conclusion with the same constructor over the larger context. The whole tactic block
elaborates to a single closed term of type \texttt{$\forall$\,\{$\Gamma$ $\varphi$\}, ND $\Gamma$ $\varphi$ $\to$ $\forall$\,\{$\Delta$\}, $\Gamma\subseteq\Delta$ $\to$ ND $\Delta$ $\varphi$}.

\subsection{Double-negation introduction}
\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:dni}
If $\Gamma \Provnd \varphi$ then $\Gamma \Provnd \Not\Not\varphi$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
To prove $\Gamma\Provnd\Not(\Not\varphi)$ apply $\Not\mathrm{I}$; it suffices to derive
$\Gamma,\Not\varphi \Provnd \bot$. By \texttt{hyp}, $\Gamma,\Not\varphi \Provnd \Not\varphi$; by
weakening of the hypothesis $\Gamma\Provnd\varphi$, also $\Gamma,\Not\varphi \Provnd \varphi$.
Then $\Not\mathrm{E}$ yields $\bot$.
\end{proof}

\lstinputlisting[linerange=dni-dni]{Thesis/Prop/NaturalDeduction.lean}

\subsection{Classical proof by cases}
The next lemma is the engine of the atom-discharge step (Section~\ref{sec:discharge}). It is the
``law of excluded middle, used silently'': we never form a disjunction object, we just split the
proof.

\begin{lemma}[Derived case split]
\label{lem:bycases}
If $\Gamma,\varphi \Provnd \chi$ and $\Gamma,\Not\varphi \Provnd \chi$, then $\Gamma \Provnd \chi$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Apply RAA: it suffices to derive $\Gamma,\Not\chi \Provnd \bot$. We build two subderivations.

\emph{(i)} $\Gamma,\Not\chi \Provnd \Not\varphi$. Apply $\Not\mathrm{I}$; we need
$\Gamma,\Not\chi,\varphi \Provnd \bot$. By weakening of the first hypothesis,
$\Gamma,\Not\chi,\varphi \Provnd \chi$; by \texttt{hyp}, $\Gamma,\Not\chi,\varphi \Provnd \Not\chi$.
Then $\Not\mathrm{E}$ gives $\bot$.

\emph{(ii)} $\Gamma,\Not\chi \Provnd \Not\Not\varphi$. Apply $\Not\mathrm{I}$; we need
$\Gamma,\Not\chi,\Not\varphi \Provnd \bot$. By weakening of the second hypothesis,
$\Gamma,\Not\chi,\Not\varphi \Provnd \chi$; by \texttt{hyp}, the same context proves $\Not\chi$.
Then $\Not\mathrm{E}$ gives $\bot$.

Finally $\Not\mathrm{E}$ applied to (ii) and (i)---i.e.\ to $\Not\Not\varphi$ and
$\Not\varphi$---yields $\Gamma,\Not\chi \Provnd \bot$, completing the reductio.
\end{proof}

\lstinputlisting[linerange=byCases-byCases]{Thesis/Prop/NaturalDeduction.lean}

\paragraph{Internal view (CIC).} \texttt{dni} and \texttt{byCases} are \emph{not} inductions: each
directly assembles an \texttt{ND}-term from constructors. For instance \texttt{dni d} elaborates to
\[
\texttt{ND.negI}\ \big(\texttt{ND.negE}\ (\texttt{ND.hyp}\ \dots)\ (\texttt{weakening } d\ \dots)\big),
\]
a constructor tree whose leaves are \texttt{hyp} and the (recursively built) weakening of $d$. The
\texttt{have e1 \dots := by \dots} blocks in \texttt{byCases} build the two subderivations as named
subterms, and \texttt{exact ND.negE e2 e1} plugs them into the $\Not\mathrm{E}$ constructor. Thus
these ``derived rules'' are admissible \emph{by construction}: each is a function returning a
genuine derivation, not a new primitive of the calculus.

\section{Soundness and consistency}
\label{sec:soundness}

\subsection{Mathematics}
Write $v \models \Gamma$ for ``$\sem{\psi}_v$ holds for every $\psi\in\Gamma$.''

\begin{lemma}[Satisfaction of an extended context]
\label{lem:satinsert}
If $\sem{\chi}_v$ holds and $v\models\Gamma$, then $v\models \Gamma,\chi$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A member of $\Gamma,\chi$ is either $\chi$ (use the first hypothesis) or a member of $\Gamma$ (use
the second).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Soundness]
\label{thm:soundness}
If $\Gamma \Provnd \varphi$, then for every valuation $v$ with $v\models\Gamma$ we have
$\sem{\varphi}_v$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By rule induction on $\Gamma\Provnd\varphi$. Fix $v$ with $v\models\Gamma$.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{hyp} ($\varphi\in\Gamma$): then $\sem{\varphi}_v$ holds since $v\models\Gamma$.
\item \textbf{$\Imp\mathrm{I}$} (from $\Gamma,\varphi\Provnd\psi$): to show
$\sem{\varphi\Imp\psi}_v = (\sem{\varphi}_v\Imp\sem{\psi}_v)$, assume $\sem{\varphi}_v$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:satinsert}, $v\models\Gamma,\varphi$; the induction hypothesis gives
$\sem{\psi}_v$.
\item \textbf{$\Imp\mathrm{E}$:} the hypotheses give $\sem{\varphi}_v\Imp\sem{\psi}_v$ and
$\sem{\varphi}_v$; modus ponens at the meta-level gives $\sem{\psi}_v$.
\item \textbf{$\Not\mathrm{I}$} (from $\Gamma,\varphi\Provnd\bot$): to show
$\sem{\Not\varphi}_v=\Not\sem{\varphi}_v$, assume $\sem{\varphi}_v$. Then $v\models\Gamma,\varphi$,
and the induction hypothesis gives $\sem{\bot}_v$, which is $\mathrm{False}$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:evalBot}.
\item \textbf{$\Not\mathrm{E}$:} the hypotheses give $\Not\sem{\varphi}_v$ and $\sem{\varphi}_v$,
hence $\mathrm{False}=\sem{\bot}_v$.
\item \textbf{$\bot\mathrm{E}$:} the hypothesis gives $\sem{\bot}_v=\mathrm{False}$, from which any
$\sem{\varphi}_v$ follows.
\item \textbf{RAA} (from $\Gamma,\Not\varphi\Provnd\bot$): to show $\sem{\varphi}_v$, suppose not;
then $\Not\sem{\varphi}_v = \sem{\Not\varphi}_v$ holds, so $v\models\Gamma,\Not\varphi$, and the
induction hypothesis gives $\sem{\bot}_v=\mathrm{False}$, a contradiction. (This is the only case
using classical logic at the meta-level.)
\end{itemize}
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Theorems are tautologies]
\label{cor:taut}
If $\emptyset \Provnd \varphi$ then $\mathrm{Taut}(\varphi)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:soundness} with $\Gamma=\emptyset$; the hypothesis $v\models\emptyset$ is
vacuous, so $\sem{\varphi}_v$ holds for every $v$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Consistency]
\label{cor:consistency}
$\bot$ is not derivable from the empty context: $\;\Not(\emptyset \Provnd \bot)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
If $\emptyset\Provnd\bot$, then by Corollary~\ref{cor:taut} $\bot$ would be a tautology; but
$\sem{\bot}_v=\mathrm{False}$ for any $v$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:evalBot}), a contradiction.
\end{proof}

audit with those constants.

\section{Completeness via Kalm\'ar's lemma (\texttt{Completeness.lean})}
\label{sec:completeness}

\subsection{Strategy}
\label{sec:strategy}
We prove: every tautology is derivable from $\emptyset$. The classical Kalm\'ar
argument~\cite{Mendelson2015,Kalmar1935} proceeds in two stages.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Per-valuation derivation (Kalm\'ar's lemma).} Fix a valuation $v$ and let
$\Gamma_v$ be the \emph{literal context} of $v$: for each atom $p$ relevant to $\varphi$, include
the literal $p$ if $v(p)=\mathsf{T}$ and $\Not p$ if $v(p)=\mathsf{F}$. We show, by induction on
$\varphi$, that $\Gamma_v$ \emph{proves} $\varphi$ when $\sem{\varphi}_v$ is true, and proves
$\Not\varphi$ when it is false. Intuitively, the derivation ``follows the truth table row $v$.''
\item \textbf{Discharging the atoms.} If $\varphi$ is a tautology, then $\sem{\varphi}_v$ is true
for every $v$, so by stage~1, \emph{every} literal context proves $\varphi$. We then eliminate the
atoms one at a time: for a fixed atom $p$, the two contexts that differ only in containing $p$
versus $\Not p$ both prove $\varphi$, so by the derived case split (Lemma~\ref{lem:bycases}) the
context \emph{without} any assumption about $p$ proves $\varphi$. Iterating over all atoms
collapses the context to $\emptyset$.
\end{enumerate}

No maximal-consistent-set / Lindenbaum machinery is needed: the argument is finitary, driven by
the finite list of atoms occurring in $\varphi$.

\subsection{Atoms, literals, and literal contexts}
\begin{definition}[Atoms of a formula]
$\mathrm{atoms}(\varphi)$ is the list of atoms occurring in $\varphi$ (with repetition):
\[
\mathrm{atoms}(p)=[p],\quad
\mathrm{atoms}(\Not\varphi)=\mathrm{atoms}(\varphi),\quad
\mathrm{atoms}(\varphi\Imp\psi)=\mathrm{atoms}(\varphi)\,{+}\!{+}\,\mathrm{atoms}(\psi).
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Literal and literal context]
For a valuation $v$ and atom $p$,
\[
\mathrm{lit}_v(p) \;=\;
\begin{cases}
p & \text{if } v(p)=\mathsf{T},\\
\Not p & \text{if } v(p)=\mathsf{F}.
\end{cases}
\]
For a list of atoms $\mathit{ats}$, the \emph{literal context} is
\[
\Gamma_v(\mathit{ats}) \;=\; \{\, \mathrm{lit}_v(p) : p \in \mathit{ats} \,\},
\]
defined by recursion: $\Gamma_v([])=\emptyset$ and
$\Gamma_v(p::\mathit{rest}) = \{\mathrm{lit}_v(p)\}\cup \Gamma_v(\mathit{rest})$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Literal membership]
\label{lem:litmem}
If $p \in \mathit{ats}$ then $\mathrm{lit}_v(p)\in\Gamma_v(\mathit{ats})$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Induction on $\mathit{ats}$: in the head case $p$ is the head and its literal is the inserted
element; in the tail case apply the induction hypothesis and the inclusion
$\Gamma_v(\mathit{rest})\subseteq \Gamma_v(p'::\mathit{rest})$.
\end{proof}

\lstinputlisting[linerange=atomsLits-atomsLits]{Thesis/Prop/Completeness.lean}

\noindent (\texttt{lit} and \texttt{litCtx} are \texttt{noncomputable} because the test ``$v\,p$''
is a proposition; under the local \texttt{Classical.propDecidable} instance the \texttt{if} is
well defined.)

\subsection{Kalm\'ar's lemma}
\begin{lemma}[Kalm\'ar]
\label{lem:kalmar}
Fix a valuation $v$ and a list $\mathit{ats}$ with $\mathrm{atoms}(\varphi)\subseteq\mathit{ats}$.
Then
\[
\big(\sem{\varphi}_v \Rightarrow \Gamma_v(\mathit{ats})\Provnd\varphi\big)
\quad\text{and}\quad
\big(\Not\sem{\varphi}_v \Rightarrow \Gamma_v(\mathit{ats})\Provnd\Not\varphi\big).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By induction on $\varphi$. Throughout write $\Gamma := \Gamma_v(\mathit{ats})$.

\paragraph{Atom $\varphi=s$.} Here $\mathrm{atoms}(s)=[s]\subseteq\mathit{ats}$, so
$s\in\mathit{ats}$ and $\mathrm{lit}_v(s)\in\Gamma$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:litmem}.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $\sem{s}_v$ (i.e.\ $v(s)=\mathsf{T}$): then $\mathrm{lit}_v(s)=s$, so $s\in\Gamma$ and
\texttt{hyp} gives $\Gamma\Provnd s$.
\item If $\Not\sem{s}_v$: then $\mathrm{lit}_v(s)=\Not s\in\Gamma$, and \texttt{hyp} gives
$\Gamma\Provnd\Not s$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Negation $\varphi=\Not p$.} Here $\mathrm{atoms}(\Not p)=\mathrm{atoms}(p)\subseteq
\mathit{ats}$, and the induction hypothesis for $p$ gives both implications.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $\sem{\Not p}_v$, i.e.\ $\Not\sem{p}_v$: the negative half of the induction hypothesis
yields $\Gamma\Provnd\Not p$, which is the goal.
\item If $\Not\sem{\Not p}_v$, i.e.\ $\Not\Not\sem{p}_v$: classically $\sem{p}_v$, so the positive
half yields $\Gamma\Provnd p$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:dni} (double-negation introduction) gives
$\Gamma\Provnd\Not\Not p$, the goal.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Implication $\varphi=p\Imp q$.} Here
$\mathrm{atoms}(p\Imp q)=\mathrm{atoms}(p)\,{+}\!{+}\,\mathrm{atoms}(q)\subseteq\mathit{ats}$, so
both $\mathrm{atoms}(p)\subseteq\mathit{ats}$ and $\mathrm{atoms}(q)\subseteq\mathit{ats}$, and we
have induction hypotheses for $p$ and $q$.

\emph{Positive half: $\sem{p\Imp q}_v \Rightarrow \Gamma\Provnd p\Imp q$.} Split on $\sem{p}_v$.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $\sem{p}_v$: then $\sem{q}_v$ (from $\sem{p\Imp q}_v$ applied to $\sem{p}_v$). The
positive half for $q$ gives $\Gamma\Provnd q$; weaken to $\Gamma,p\Provnd q$ and apply
$\Imp\mathrm{I}$ to get $\Gamma\Provnd p\Imp q$.
\item If $\Not\sem{p}_v$: the negative half for $p$ gives $\Gamma\Provnd\Not p$. Apply
$\Imp\mathrm{I}$; we must derive $\Gamma,p\Provnd q$. By weakening, $\Gamma,p\Provnd\Not p$, and by
\texttt{hyp}, $\Gamma,p\Provnd p$; then $\Not\mathrm{E}$ gives $\Gamma,p\Provnd\bot$ and
$\bot\mathrm{E}$ gives $\Gamma,p\Provnd q$.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Negative half: $\Not\sem{p\Imp q}_v \Rightarrow \Gamma\Provnd\Not(p\Imp q)$.} From
$\Not(\sem{p}_v\Imp\sem{q}_v)$ we obtain classically that $\sem{p}_v$ holds (otherwise the
implication would be vacuously true) and $\sem{q}_v$ fails (otherwise the implication would hold).
The positive half for $p$ gives $\Gamma\Provnd p$; the negative half for $q$ gives
$\Gamma\Provnd\Not q$. Apply $\Not\mathrm{I}$; we must derive $\Gamma,(p\Imp q)\Provnd\bot$. By
\texttt{hyp}, $\Gamma,(p\Imp q)\Provnd p\Imp q$; by weakening, $\Gamma,(p\Imp q)\Provnd p$; then
$\Imp\mathrm{E}$ gives $\Gamma,(p\Imp q)\Provnd q$. By weakening, $\Gamma,(p\Imp q)\Provnd\Not q$;
then $\Not\mathrm{E}$ gives $\bot$.
\end{proof}

\lstinputlisting[linerange=kalmar-kalmar]{Thesis/Prop/Completeness.lean}

\noindent The code follows the proof case-for-case: \texttt{not\_not.1} is the classical step in
the negation case; \texttt{by\_cases hp : eval v p} is the split in the positive implication case;
the two \texttt{by\_contra}/\texttt{absurd} lines extract $\sem{p}_v$ and $\Not\sem{q}_v$ in the
negative implication case.

\paragraph{Internal view (CIC).} Here \texttt{induction $\varphi$} is \texttt{Formula.rec} (not
\texttt{ND.rec}): we recurse on the \emph{syntax}, with motive
\[
C\,\varphi \ :=\ \texttt{atoms } \varphi \subseteq \mathit{ats}\ \to\ \big(\texttt{eval } v\,\varphi \to \texttt{ND } \Gamma\,\varphi\big)\ \wedge\ \big(\neg\,\texttt{eval } v\,\varphi \to \texttt{ND } \Gamma\,(\Not\varphi)\big).
\]
In the \texttt{neg} and \texttt{impl} branches the recursor supplies the induction hypotheses
\texttt{ih}, \texttt{ihp}, \texttt{ihq} for the immediate subformulas; the proof \emph{consumes}
those (meta-level) hypotheses and \emph{produces} \texttt{ND}-terms with constructors
(\texttt{ND.impI}, \texttt{ND.negI}, \texttt{ND.botE}, \texttt{ND.negE}, \dots) and the derived
rule \texttt{dni}. So a single run of \texttt{Formula.rec} turns the truth value of $\varphi$ under
$v$ into an actual derivation tree---this is the ``completeness as a program'' content of
Lemma~\ref{lem:kalmar} made literal.

\subsection{The literal context depends only on truth values}
To discharge an atom we will perturb the valuation at that atom; we must know this does not
disturb the context built from the \emph{other} atoms.

\begin{lemma}[Congruence of literals and contexts]
\label{lem:congr}
If two valuations $w_1,w_2$ agree on an atom $x$ (i.e.\ $w_1(x)\leftrightarrow w_2(x)$), then
$\mathrm{lit}_{w_1}(x)=\mathrm{lit}_{w_2}(x)$. Consequently, if $w_1,w_2$ agree on every atom of a
list $\mathit{ats}$, then $\Gamma_{w_1}(\mathit{ats})=\Gamma_{w_2}(\mathit{ats})$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For the first claim, both literals are $x$ if the common value is $\mathsf{T}$ and $\Not x$
otherwise. The second is induction on $\mathit{ats}$, applying the first claim to the head and the
induction hypothesis to the tail.
\end{proof}

\lstinputlisting[linerange=congr-congr]{Thesis/Prop/Completeness.lean}

\subsection{Discharging the atoms}
\label{sec:discharge}
\begin{lemma}[Discharge]
\label{lem:discharge}
Let $\mathit{ats}$ be a list of atoms with no repetitions. If $\Gamma_v(\mathit{ats})\Provnd\varphi$
holds for \emph{every} valuation $v$, then $\emptyset\Provnd\varphi$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By induction on $\mathit{ats}$.

\paragraph{Empty list.} $\Gamma_v([])=\emptyset$ for every $v$, so the hypothesis instantiated at
any valuation already gives $\emptyset\Provnd\varphi$.

\paragraph{Cons $p::\mathit{rest}$} (with $p\notin\mathit{rest}$ and $\mathit{rest}$ repetition-free).
It suffices, by the induction hypothesis for $\mathit{rest}$, to show
$\Gamma_v(\mathit{rest})\Provnd\varphi$ for an arbitrary $v$. Consider the two valuations
$v[p{:=}\mathsf{T}]$ and $v[p{:=}\mathsf{F}]$ obtained from $v$ by overriding the value at $p$.
\begin{itemize}
\item Under $v[p{:=}\mathsf{T}]$ the literal of $p$ is $p$; and since $v[p{:=}\mathsf{T}]$ agrees
with $v$ on every atom of $\mathit{rest}$ (as $p\notin\mathit{rest}$),
Lemma~\ref{lem:congr} gives $\Gamma_{v[p:=\mathsf{T}]}(\mathit{rest})=\Gamma_v(\mathit{rest})$.
Hence the hypothesis at $v[p{:=}\mathsf{T}]$ reads
$\{p\}\cup\Gamma_v(\mathit{rest})\Provnd\varphi$; call this $d_1$.
\item Symmetrically, under $v[p{:=}\mathsf{F}]$ the literal is $\Not p$, and the hypothesis reads
$\{\Not p\}\cup\Gamma_v(\mathit{rest})\Provnd\varphi$; call this $d_2$.
\end{itemize}
By the derived case split (Lemma~\ref{lem:bycases}) applied to $d_1$ and $d_2$, we obtain
$\Gamma_v(\mathit{rest})\Provnd\varphi$, as required.
\end{proof}

\lstinputlisting[linerange=discharge-discharge]{Thesis/Prop/Completeness.lean}

\noindent \texttt{Function.update v p True} is $v[p{:=}\mathsf{T}]$; \texttt{hctx\_t}/\texttt{hctx\_f}
are the applications of Lemma~\ref{lem:congr} (the side condition $x\neq p$ comes from
$p\notin\mathit{rest}$, i.e.\ \texttt{hpr}); the final \texttt{byCases d1 d2} is the case split.

\subsection{The completeness theorem}
\begin{theorem}[Completeness of ND]
\label{thm:completeness}
Every tautology is derivable from the empty context:
$\;\mathrm{Taut}(\varphi)\Rightarrow \emptyset\Provnd\varphi$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $\mathit{ats}$ be $\mathrm{atoms}(\varphi)$ with duplicates removed; then $\mathit{ats}$ is
repetition-free and still satisfies $\mathrm{atoms}(\varphi)\subseteq\mathit{ats}$. Fix any
valuation $v$. Since $\varphi$ is a tautology, $\sem{\varphi}_v$ holds, so the positive half of
Kalm\'ar's lemma (Lemma~\ref{lem:kalmar}) gives $\Gamma_v(\mathit{ats})\Provnd\varphi$. As $v$ was
arbitrary, the hypothesis of the discharge lemma (Lemma~\ref{lem:discharge}) is met, and we
conclude $\emptyset\Provnd\varphi$.
\end{proof}

\lstinputlisting[linerange=completeness-completeness]{Thesis/Prop/Completeness.lean}

\subsection{Soundness and completeness together}
\begin{theorem}[Adequacy]
\label{thm:adequacy}
For every formula $\varphi$, $\;\mathrm{Taut}(\varphi)\ \leftrightarrow\ \emptyset\Provnd\varphi$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Left-to-right is Theorem~\ref{thm:completeness}; right-to-left is Corollary~\ref{cor:taut}.
\end{proof}

\lstinputlisting[linerange=soundComplete-soundComplete]{Thesis/Prop/Completeness.lean}

\noindent Theorem~\ref{thm:adequacy} is the precise formal statement of the thesis goal:
\emph{derivability in propositional natural deduction coincides exactly with semantic validity},
proved in Lean from the genuine inference rules.

\paragraph{Internal view (CIC).} \texttt{completeness\_ND} is a closed term of the $\Pi$-type
\[
\forall (\varphi : \texttt{Formula}),\ \texttt{IsTautology } \varphi\ \to\ \texttt{ND } \emptyset\ \varphi,
\]
obtained by composing \texttt{discharge}, \texttt{kalmar}, and lemmas on \texttt{dedup}. The
biconditional \texttt{soundComplete} is the term \texttt{$\langle$completeness\_ND $\varphi$,
isTautology\_of\_provable$\rangle$}: in CIC, \texttt{Iff} is a structure with two fields
(the two implications), and the anonymous-constructor notation $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ builds
its inhabitant. There is no remaining proof obligation once the kernel type-checks this term
against the stated type---type-checking \emph{is} the verification of adequacy.

\section{Verification: the axiom audit}
\label{sec:axioms}

A formalization is only as honest as its dependencies. Two failure modes must be excluded:
an admitted gap (\texttt{sorry}) and a bespoke postulated rule (an ``oracle''). Lean's
\texttt{\#print axioms} command reports the complete set of logical axioms a theorem transitively
depends on; a \texttt{sorry} surfaces as the pseudo-axiom \texttt{sorryAx}, and any postulated
constant would appear by name. Running it on the main results yields:

\lstinputlisting[language={},basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]{audit.txt}

The three axioms reported---\texttt{propext} (propositional extensionality),
\texttt{Classical.choice} (the axiom of choice), and \texttt{Quot.sound} (soundness of
quotients)---are precisely the standard classical foundations on which all of Lean's Mathlib is
built; they are not specific to this development. In particular:
\begin{itemize}
\item No \texttt{sorryAx} appears: there are no admitted gaps.
\item No constructor or constant named \texttt{oracle} (or any library completeness theorem)
appears: completeness is genuinely internal.
\item The hand-built derivations \texttt{ex\_id} and \texttt{ex\_dne} depend on \emph{no} axioms at
all, since they are finite proof terms in the inductive type \texttt{ND}.
\end{itemize}
This is the formal certificate that Theorem~\ref{thm:adequacy} is exactly what it claims to be.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have given a complete, kernel-checked proof of the adequacy of classical propositional natural
deduction:
\[
\mathrm{Taut}(\varphi)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \emptyset\Provnd\varphi .
\]
Soundness is a routine rule induction (Theorem~\ref{thm:soundness}); completeness is the
Kalm\'ar argument carried out entirely with the system's own inference rules
(Theorem~\ref{thm:completeness}), via a per-valuation construction (Lemma~\ref{lem:kalmar}) and a
finite atom-discharge step (Lemma~\ref{lem:discharge}) powered by a derived classical case split
(Lemma~\ref{lem:bycases}). The axiom audit (Section~\ref{sec:axioms}) certifies the proof is free
of admitted gaps and of any postulated completeness rule.

Conceptually, the development realizes Stansifer's slogan---``completeness as a program''---in the
natural-deduction setting: the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:kalmar} is an effective procedure that,
given the truth table of $\varphi$, assembles a concrete derivation, which the discharge step then
folds into a derivation from the empty context. Appendix~\ref{app:foundation} provides an
\emph{independent} cross-check of the same metatheorem through the \emph{Foundation} library,
strengthening confidence that the surface semantics is the intended one.

\section{Cross-validation against the Foundation library}
\label{app:foundation}

The completeness theorem of Section~\ref{sec:completeness} stands on its own. Independently, we
verified the same semantic adequacy through the \emph{Foundation} library
(\texttt{FormalizedFormalLogic})~\cite{FoundationRepo}, which already contains a completeness
theorem for a classical propositional calculus. This appendix records that cross-check. It is
deliberately \emph{not} imported by the main development: the full Mathlib closure used by our core
and the full closure used by Foundation clash on a duplicate \texttt{Matrix.map} declaration, so
the appendix builds as a separate Lake target (\texttt{Thesis.Prop.CompletenessViaFoundation}).
Nothing in Sections~\ref{sec:syntax}--\ref{sec:axioms} depends on it.

\subsection{Translation into Foundation syntax}
Foundation's classical calculus is presented over negation-normal formulas, so we translate our
surface syntax using the classical identity $(\varphi\Imp\psi)\equiv(\Not\varphi\Or\psi)$:
\[
t(p)=p,\qquad t(\Not\varphi)=\Not t(\varphi),\qquad t(\varphi\Imp\psi)=\Not t(\varphi)\Or t(\psi).
\]

\lstinputlisting[linerange=tr-tr]{Thesis/Prop/BridgeToFoundation.lean}

\subsection{The bridge lemma}
\begin{lemma}[Semantics preservation]\label{lem:bridge}
For every valuation $v$ and surface formula $\varphi$, $\;\sem{\varphi}_v \leftrightarrow
\mathrm{Sat}(v, t(\varphi))$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Structural induction on $\varphi$. The atom and negation cases unfold definitionally; the
implication case reduces, after unfolding, to the classical tautology
$(P\Imp Q)\leftrightarrow(\Not P\Or Q)$, discharged by \texttt{tauto}.
\end{proof}

\lstinputlisting[linerange=evalTr-evalTr]{Thesis/Prop/BridgeLemma.lean}

\subsection{Importing Foundation's completeness}
Composing the bridge lemma with the library's completeness theorem gives, for any surface
tautology $\varphi$, a Foundation proof of $t(\varphi)$ from the empty theory. This furnishes an
independent confirmation that our surface notion of tautology matches the library's, and hence an
external corroboration of Theorem~\ref{thm:adequacy}.
